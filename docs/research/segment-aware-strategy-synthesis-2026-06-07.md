## Part 1 — Executive Summary

**The insight.** TeaAgent has an opportunity that none of the eight competitor tools examined have taken: building **proactive, transparent, format-aware chunking** as a first-class, governed capability — not a silent compaction mechanism, not a reactive error message, but a *proposal* the user can inspect, redirect, or decline, executed with the same audit trail and cost discipline teaAgent already applies to destructive tool calls.

**Why now.** Three independent bodies of evidence converge on this moment:
- *The trigger is real and recent*: the JSON-truncation bug documented in `docs/debugging/json-parsing-bug-diagnosis-2026-06-07.md` is a concrete, dated instance of the exact failure mode this document addresses — a payload exceeded the usable token budget, was cut mid-structure, and broke downstream parsing, with nothing upstream ever asking "will this fit?"
- *Competitors have not closed this gap, and users are visibly asking for it*: across eight tools and nine real GitHub issues, not one ships a user-facing "this is large — want me to split it up?" proposal. The feature has been requested, by name and by description, against Aider, Cline, and Claude Code, and none of those requests have shipped.
- *The research community has moved past "give it more tokens"*: 2025–2026 work (Chain of Agents, ACON, the Chroma context-rot study, the Noise Decomposition framework, a 37-author survey) treats decomposition and compression as first-tier open problems, actively dismantling the assumption that bigger context windows solve the underlying issue.

**TeaAgent's advantage.** This is not a feature teaAgent would be bolting onto a blank slate. The design research found that teaAgent already has the exact substrate this capability needs: `ContextPressureScore` and `TokenBudgetReport` that *measure* pressure, an `ApprovalBackend`/`PromptBackend` JIT-consent flow that already routes consequential decisions through a single, audited channel, a `RunStore` audit trail, a cancel-token infrastructure, and a cost-tracking system that recently received attention (default cost rates for ollama/vllm/fake, per the project's recent fix history). Segment-aware processing is not a new trust model bolted onto teaAgent — it is the *existing* trust model, applied to a new class of decision.

**Timeline (from the implementation design, recast as phases rather than calendar dates — see the caveat in Part 5).** A visibility-only MVP (detect overflow, propose, no chunking yet) is the smallest valuable slice and could plausibly land first; a single format done well (the design research recommends starting with JSON or logs, given the triggering bug) is the next milestone; broader format coverage and aggregation sophistication follow only once real usage data exists. **Important: none of the four source documents commits to a hard calendar.** The "MVP in 6 weeks, production in 12 weeks" framing in the original brief for this synthesis is *not* a finding from the research — it is a planning assumption that the research explicitly warns against manufacturing without velocity data. This synthesis treats it as a placeholder to be replaced with a real estimate once someone with visibility into teaAgent's actual development cadence reviews the phased plan in Part 5.

**Success metric.** The clearest framing to emerge from the research, synthesized across all four documents, is not "process arbitrarily large inputs" (a capability claim that invites an arms race teaAgent cannot win against larger-context-window competitors) but **"never let a user be surprised — by a truncated answer, an unexpected bill, or a silent loss of information — when a task is too big for one pass."** That is a governance claim, not a capacity claim, and it is the one place the research consistently shows competitors failing.

## Part 2 — What We Learned from Research

### From the Chunking & Long-Context Literature Review

The combined research pass (`segment-aware-agent-strategy-2026-06-07.md`) assembled an annotated bibliography of 24 sources, each independently fetched and read — not generated from memory. Three findings matter most for a design decision:

- **There is no universal best chunking strategy.** Pinecone's 2025 survey (source #1 in the bibliography) is explicit that the right approach depends on data type, embedding model, and query shape. This is not a hedge; it is the single strongest argument against building five format-specific chunkers speculatively before knowing which one teaAgent's real workloads actually need.
- **"Partition along existing structure before falling back to brute-force splitting" is the one meta-principle the literature converges on.** Unstructured's two-stage "partition into elements, then split only as a last resort" approach, LangChain's `RecursiveJsonSplitter` (a *real, shipped* JSON-aware chunker — the closest existing prior art to what the original brief asked for), and AST-based code chunking all independently arrive at the same shape: respect the format's own boundaries first.
- **Advertised context size is not a reliable design constant.** NVIDIA's RULER benchmark found that roughly half of models claiming ≥32K context cannot maintain satisfactory performance even at that length, and a November 2025 *Understanding AI* analysis cites Claude 3.5 Sonnet's long-document inference accuracy falling from 88% to 30% as context filled. Any `ContextAnalyzer` that uses the model's nominal window as its overflow threshold is, per this evidence, being too generous.

**On the question the synthesis brief specifically asked about — "could Reddit/Discord/X sentiment be verified?"** The answer from the strategy document is an unambiguous *no*, stated as a methodological disclosure rather than papered over: every `site:reddit.com` search returned no indexed results, direct fetches of reddit.com were refused by the fetch tool, and there is no tool in this environment that can authenticate into Discord or search X. The community-sentiment document (a separate research pass) handled this gap slightly differently — it reports Reddit sentiment as "reconstructed from indexed search-result summaries... marked '(via search index)'" rather than omitted outright. **This is a real discrepancy between the two source documents, not a contradiction this synthesis can resolve**: one treated indirect signal as reportable-with-caveats, the other treated it as unusable. The honest position for any document built on top of both is to treat *all* Reddit-sourced claims in this space as low-confidence, regardless of which framing produced them, and to note that **direct, authenticated access to Reddit/Discord/X remains a genuine evidence gap** that would need a person, not a research pass, to close.

### From the Competitor Audit

The competitor audit (`competitor-chunking-audit-2026-06-07.md`) examined eight tools — a more exhaustive set than the six covered inside the combined strategy document — using a deliberately narrow, falsifiable question: *does the tool detect oversized input, and if so, does it error, silently truncate, silently summarize, or proactively propose a segmented plan?*

**The headline finding, stated identically (independently) in both the competitor audit and the combined strategy document: nobody proposes chunking to the user as a first-class feature.** The audit organizes the landscape into three camps:

1. **Conversation-level compaction is now industry baseline — not a place to differentiate.** Claude Code, Cline, and Copilot CLI have independently converged on near-identical "monitor usage against a threshold, generate an LLM-written summary, splice it in to replace history" mechanisms, and all three vendors' own documentation admits this is lossy. The audit's explicit advice: *replicate this so as not to look behind, but do not market it as an advantage.*
2. **Single oversized files are uniformly weak-to-broken — this is the gap.** Across five of eight tools (Claude Code, OpenCode, Cline, Copilot Chat, Aider), the pattern is identical: the maintainers know about the problem (it is filed, discussed, sometimes consolidated into tracking issues), but **genuine chunked, progressive, or streaming reads of one large file have not shipped in any of them.** Concrete evidence: Claude Code's hardcoded ~25K-token tool-result cap was confirmed *not user-configurable* in a maintainer-closed issue (#45770, "not planned"); Cline added a flat ~300KB block on file reads that ignores a model's actual capacity (a 657KB file that would fit comfortably in a 1M-token Gemini window is simply refused); OpenCode's truncation messages "instruct agents to use tools that aren't available," producing un-escapable "doom loops" (#18108); Copilot Chat's `#file` directive silently omits files that don't fit. **Aider is the one outlier in honesty**: its troubleshooting docs tell the user, twice, in plain language, to "break your code into smaller source files" — proposing chunking as a *manual human task*, not something the tool does.
3. **RAG frameworks (LangChain, LlamaIndex) solved chunking years ago — but only at indexing time, decoupled from the live agent loop.** LlamaIndex in particular has the most mature *philosophy* of context preservation found anywhere in the survey (`SentenceWindowNodeParser`, `HierarchicalNodeParser`/`AutoMergingRetriever` with a 2048/512/128-character tier hierarchy) — but it is an indexing-pipeline feature, not something that activates when an agent's live context is about to overflow mid-task. CrewAI, notably, has *no* opinion on the subject at all and crashes outright via `litellm.ContextWindowExceededError`.

The audit's own "Classification" section is unusually direct about what this means for a build decision: *"Transparent, user-controllable, progressive/chunked reading of individual oversized files... [is] the open gap nobody has closed."* And its "Handoff" section adds an important caution worth repeating verbatim in spirit: **don't claim differentiation on a feature that doesn't yet exist — build it first, then cite this audit as the "nobody else does this well" baseline.**

### From the Community Sentiment Analysis

The community-sentiment document (`community-sentiment-chunking-2026-06-07.md`) gathered direct quotes from nine GitHub issues across six projects (Aider, OpenCode, LangChain, gpt-engineer, AnythingLLM, Copilot CLI), two Hacker News threads, and a cross-section of 2024–2026 arXiv papers. Five findings are load-bearing:

- **The dominant theme is the gap between *advertised* and *usable* context — and a desire for honesty about that gap, not just bigger numbers.** A user running a 262K-capable model on a 24GB GPU explicitly asked Aider for a setting to *artificially cap* context lower than the model claims, because real usable capacity collapses well before the spec-sheet number (Aider #4583, open with no maintainer response). This is not an isolated complaint — it is corroborated independently by the Chroma "context rot" research finding that a 200K-window model can show meaningful degradation by 50K tokens.
- **Eager inlining is the default; lazy, on-demand chunk retrieval is still a feature request, not a shipped behavior.** OpenCode's `AGENTS.md` bug (#18037) is the starkest illustration: a 331KB instructions file consumed 81% of a 128K context window *before the agent did anything*, and the user's proposed fix — fetch large static content on demand rather than inlining it — is exactly the "addressable chunks" pattern researchers recommend as best practice.
- **Even the bookkeeping beneath chunking is unreliable in shipping tools, which undermines any strategy built on top of it.** OpenCode #15871 found compaction firing at ~144K tokens against a configured 1M-token model because of a hardcoded `contextWindow: 200000` fallback constant; AnythingLLM #4905 documents a configured 96,000-token window silently collapsing to 8,192 mid-task. **This is a sharp, practical warning for teaAgent**: before building a `ChunkingProposal` on top of `ContextAnalyzer`'s overflow estimate, the estimate itself has to be trustworthy — competitors have shipped chunking-adjacent features on top of broken accounting and paid for it in user trust.
- **Chunk *assembly* — ordering, coherence, placement — is almost entirely off the radar of the tooling community, even though research says it's a first-class lever.** Chroma's most counter-intuitive finding (shuffled, less-coherent context sometimes *outperforms* a logically-ordered one) and Veseli et al.'s refinement of "lost in the middle" (recency dominates past 50% fill) both imply that *how* reassembled chunks are ordered matters as much as *which* chunks are kept — yet not one of the GitHub issues surveyed mentions chunk ordering as a tunable. This is a place teaAgent could differentiate on substance, not just process.
- **Sentiment is genuinely bifurcated, and the document is explicit about *why*.** Among practitioners (GitHub issues, dev blogs): "medium enthusiasm, high frustration" — the emotional register is closer to resignation ("does someone have a clue what im doing wrong?") than excitement, and several well-reasoned proposals (LangChain #2257, gpt-engineer #759) were closed or simply died. Among researchers: "high and rising" — a wave of 2025–2026 work treats this as a first-tier open problem. On Hacker News specifically, the split is explicit and quotable: one commenter called chunking "the highest leverage thing someone can work on right now," while another dismissed it ("Chunking is less important in the long context era with most people just pulling in top 20K"). **The practitioner fatigue and the researcher urgency are not in tension — they describe the same gap from two different vantage points**: the people closest to the pain have stopped expecting a fix; the people studying the problem are discovering it's deeper than anyone assumed.

### From the Implementation Design

The design document (`segment-aware-agent-design-2026-06-07.md`) is the most directly actionable of the four, because — unlike the others — it is grounded in a specific reading of teaAgent's own codebase rather than external research. Its central finding: **the missing layer is narrow and well-bounded.** TeaAgent already measures context pressure on the *workspace* (`ContextPressureScore` in `teaagent/context_pressure.py`, `TokenBudgetReport` in `teaagent/daily.py`, `compute_context_health` in `teaagent/context_health.py`); what it lacks is a pre-flight analyzer that estimates whether a single **task input** will overflow *before* the agent attempts it.

Five things from this document carry the most design weight:

1. **The proposal flow is not a new UX pattern — it's the existing JIT-approval pattern, applied to a new class of decision.** The design explicitly maps `ChunkingProposal.present()` onto the same `ApprovalBackend`/`PromptBackend` channel that destructive tool calls already route through, which means it shows up consistently in TUI, headless, and scripted modes, and respects `--permission-mode` the same way every other consequential decision does. This is the single most important architectural decision in the whole design, because it means teaAgent doesn't need to invent user trust in a new mechanism — it can extend trust the user has already placed in an existing one.
2. **Eight core tests plus three deferred-but-flagged tests define what "done" looks like, and all are runnable without a live model.** `SegmentProcessor` and `ChunkingProposal` are designed around injectable fakes (mirroring teaAgent's existing `fake` provider pattern), so the chunking *logic* — overflow detection at the 60% threshold, JSON structural preservation, code import preservation, carryover budget compliance, structural merge correctness, failure-skip visibility, and rejection fallback — is fully unit-testable in isolation. The three deferred tests (systemic-failure abort, mid-run cancel returning a partial result, oversized-atomic-unit visible degradation) are explicitly flagged as "not blocking initial implementation, but flagged so they aren't forgotten" — a phrasing worth preserving, because it signals these are known-important, not afterthoughts.
3. **Runnable pseudocode exists for five chunkers (JSON, CSV, Code, Text, XML) — not five abstract descriptions.** Each follows one shared `Chunker` protocol (`chunk(content, target_tokens) -> list[Segment]`) and a documented atomicity guarantee (never split a JSON object's key-value pairs, never split a CSV row, never split a function body, etc.). The JSON chunker in particular injects a `_segment` envelope (`{"index": 1, "total": 30, "covers": "users[0:333]"}`) directly into each chunk — a small design choice with an outsized payoff, because it gives `ResultAggregator` a structural key to merge on rather than forcing it to guess at overlap.
4. **Four open questions are named explicitly, for maintainer review — not buried in implementation notes.** (a) Can `estimate_tokens` cleanly wrap whatever already backs `build_token_budget_report`'s estimator? (b) Should the 60% trigger and 50% chunk-size fraction live in `ContextProfile` for per-profile tuning, or be global constants? (c) Does the `ResultAggregator`'s reduce pass need a recursive/tree-reduce design for very large segment counts (100+), or is a flat reduce fine for the realistic case (~30 segments)? (d) Does `teaagent/context_pack.py`'s existing LSP hydration path expose a symbol table/call-graph primitive `CodeChunker` can build on directly?
5. **Risk analysis covers four named categories — accuracy, cost, UX, complexity — each with a stated residual risk the design *accepts* rather than claims to eliminate.** This is worth calling out because it is unusual rigor for a design document: rather than presenting mitigations as if they fully solve the problem, each risk section ends with an explicit "residual risk accepted" line (e.g., "no amount of carryover fully replaces single-shot analysis with a sufficiently large context window"). That honesty is itself evidence the design is ready for review, not oversold.

## Part 3 — Why TeaAgent Is Uniquely Positioned

### The governance-first substrate already exists

The strongest single claim this synthesis can make — and it is a claim *grounded in the design document's direct reading of the codebase*, not an aspiration — is that teaAgent does not need to build a new trust model to support segment-aware processing. It needs to **extend an existing one** to a new class of decision. Specifically:

- **Cost tracking.** The design explicitly ties the chunking-overhead disclosure (estimated at ~20–30%, see Part 6) to "teaAgent's existing cost-estimation machinery — the same family of code that produces `TokenBudgetReport`." The recent fix history (default cost rates for ollama/vllm/fake) suggests this machinery is actively maintained, not legacy code being retrofitted.
- **JIT approval / consent flow.** `ApprovalBackend` and `PromptBackend` already gate destructive or expensive actions behind explicit user consent (`reason_code='jit_required'`), and — per the design's citation of "P0-TR-001" — even `allow_all_destructive` does not bypass this gate outside of `danger-full-access` mode. The design's framing is exact: *"Chunking is the same class of decision: it changes cost, latency, and — critically — result fidelity... The user must consent to that trade-off the same way they consent to a destructive file write."*
- **Audit trail.** `RunStore` already persists approval decisions; the design proposes routing `DecisionAuditRecord` through the same store, "no new storage layer." Combined with the project's HMAC audit chain (referenced in recent fix history around "HMAC key save error handling" and "hmac deprecation fix"), this gives teaAgent something none of the eight competitors examined have: **a per-segment audit trail with cryptographic integrity guarantees.**
- **Isolation and cancellation.** The cancel-token infrastructure (`tests/integration/test_cancel_token.py`, `teaagent/tool_call_context.py`) is designed to wire directly into `SegmentProcessor`, so a mid-run cancel stops *between* segments — never mid-segment — and returns a clearly-marked partial result.

### The competitive moat this enables

Putting the competitor audit's finding ("nobody proposes chunking, and single-large-file handling is uniformly weak") next to teaAgent's existing governance substrate produces a positioning claim that is *defensible on evidence*, not aspirational marketing:

> **TeaAgent can be the only agent where every chunk of a large task runs with the same isolation, cost cap, and cryptographically-anchored audit trail as every other action it takes — because that's not a new feature, it's the existing trust model, applied consistently.**

This is harder to copy than it looks. A competitor could plausibly ship format-aware JSON chunking in a sprint — `RecursiveJsonSplitter` is real, shipped, open-source prior art they could adopt tomorrow. What they could *not* easily retrofit is a governance layer deep enough that every chunk's processing is independently auditable, cost-capped, and isolated — because, per the competitor audit, none of them built that layer for *any* of their existing actions, let alone a new one. Retrofitting governance into an architecture that wasn't designed for it is a multi-quarter undertaking; extending governance that already exists to one more decision type is comparatively narrow, bounded work — exactly the shape the design document describes in its "Section 2.1: Where this plugs into the existing run loop" analysis (a non-overflowing task never touches any of the new modules; the blast radius is contained).

### Target customer — grounded in the evidence, not invented

The research does not contain direct customer interviews or market sizing — that would require a different kind of research pass. What it *does* contain is a clear shape of who is filing the issues that describe this exact pain:

- **Users running large structured payloads through agents that silently fail or crash** (the AnythingLLM 96K→8K collapse, the CrewAI `ContextWindowExceededError` crashes, the Cline 657KB-file block) — these are the people who would immediately recognize "I can tell you exactly which chunk produced which finding, and exactly what I dropped" as solving a problem they currently have no good answer to.
- **Users who explicitly want *less* automation and more control** (Aider #4583's request to artificially cap context) — a reminder that "the agent manages this for me" is not a universal preference, and that the proposal-based design (which always offers "stop and let me rescope" as an explicit option) is a better fit for this segment than either silent compaction or a hard wall.
- **Regulated-industry and audit-conscious users** are *not* directly evidenced in any of the four research documents — this is this synthesis's own inference, built by combining the competitive-moat argument above with teaAgent's existing governance positioning (referenced in recent strategic-assessment work). It is a *plausible* target segment given what teaAgent already has, but it should be validated with real conversations before being treated as a confirmed market, not a hypothesis.

## Part 4 — Technical Design (Integrated from All Sources)

### Architecture, as specified by the design document and corroborated by the literature

```
ContextAnalyzer  →  FormatDetector  →  (cheap structural pass)  →  ChunkingProposal
                                                                          │
                                                            user accepts / redirects / declines
                                                                          │
                                          ┌───────────────────────────────┴───────────────────────┐
                                          │                                                         │
                                ChunkerRegistry → SegmentProcessor → ResultAggregator    "stop and rescope" path
                                  (5 format chunkers,    (one model call/segment,           (graceful decline —
                                   shared protocol,       carryover, retry/skip/abort        agent does NOT then
                                   plugin-registered)     ladder, cancel-token wired)        attempt the task anyway)
```

**One important refinement the strategy document makes to the brief's original proposed sequencing — and which this synthesis adopts as the recommended order**: `ChunkingProposal` should come *after* a cheap structural look, not before. The reasoning, drawn directly from the literature (Unstructured's "partition into elements first," `RecursiveJsonSplitter`'s hierarchy walk): an agent cannot propose a *good* plan without first knowing the input's shape. "I'll split this into 5 chunks" is a materially weaker proposal than "I'll split this by top-level array entries, since that's how the JSON is structured" — and the difference is exactly the kind of thing that determines whether a user trusts the proposal enough to accept it on the first read.

#### ContextAnalyzer (`teaagent/context_analyzer.py` — new)
Reuses the tokenizer/heuristic already backing `build_token_budget_report`'s `estimated_input_tokens` — explicitly "a thin reframe of an existing estimator applied to a task payload rather than the workspace," not new tokenization logic. Produces an `OverflowRisk` dataclass (`ratio`, `level: none|watch|propose|critical`, `headroom_tokens`) that mirrors the level taxonomy `ContextPressureScore.usage_level` already uses, so the TUI can render it with the same widget — zero new UI plumbing for the detection layer.

**A grounding the literature adds here that the design doesn't dwell on**: RULER's finding that roughly half of models claiming ≥32K context fail to use it reliably argues that `ContextAnalyzer`'s threshold should be calibrated against a *usable*-capacity estimate, not the model's advertised window. The design's 60% trigger (below) is one candidate operationalization of that idea, but the literature's evidence suggests the "practical ceiling" itself may need to be a per-model, empirically-tuned number — which dovetails with Open Question #2 below (should thresholds live in `ContextProfile`?).

#### ChunkingProposal (`teaagent/chunking_proposal.py` — new)
Builds a small, ordered list of `ProposalOption`s — always including "stop and let me rescope" as the last option — and routes presentation through the *existing* `ApprovalBackend`/`PromptBackend` JIT-prompt channel, so it behaves identically across TUI, headless, and scripted modes and respects `--permission-mode`. Decisions are persisted via `RunStore`, alongside the run, in the same audit trail approval decisions already get.

The wording of the proposal itself is where the literature and the design converge most tightly. The strategy document's §6.2 evaluates three candidate phrasings against the evidence and finds the middle ground wins decisively:
- *"I'll split this into 3 chunks and process each"* — too terse to object to anything specific.
- *"Large task detected. Want me to process in segments?"* — too vague to evaluate.
- ***A plan the user can actually inspect and redirect*** — e.g., "this log is ~140K tokens; I'd split it by timestamp into 5 windows of ~28K each, summarize each, then merge — does that grouping make sense, or would splitting by service name fit your question better?" — costs more to generate and read, but "buys back the trust that silent or vague approaches were shown (in the GitHub evidence) to spend."

The design document's worked example matches this third pattern closely — its mock dialogue offers three concrete, numbered options with a stated recommendation, accepts free-form replacement text routed through teaAgent's existing intent-clarification layer, and states the cost overhead (~22% more tokens) *before* the user commits, not after.

#### FormatDetector + ChunkerRegistry (`teaagent/format_detector.py`, `teaagent/chunkers/` — new)
Cheap, sniff-based format detection (extension + first-N-bytes heuristic + lightweight content classifier — must run *before* any proposal, so it cannot itself become a context cost) dispatches into a plugin-registered `ChunkerRegistry`. Five chunkers are designed in full, each implementing one shared protocol — `chunk(content, target_tokens) -> list[Segment]` — with format-specific atomicity guarantees:

| Format | Atomic unit (never split mid-…) | What's repeated in every chunk |
|---|---|---|
| JSON | …an object's key-value pairs | sibling keys (metadata, schema, config) + injected `_segment` envelope |
| CSV | …a row | the header row |
| Code | …a function body | the file's import/`using`/`require` block; clusters grouped by *call-graph adjacency*, not file order |
| Text | …a sentence (paragraph is the primary unit) | nothing structural — falls back to sentence splitting only for oversized paragraphs |
| XML | …an element's open/close tag pair | the ancestor path and any non-repeating siblings (e.g. a `<header>` block) |

Two design choices here are worth calling out as non-obvious and well-justified:
- **The code chunker clusters by call-graph adjacency, not file order**, on the reasoning that a model analyzing `process_a()` *needs* to see `helpers()` to reason about it correctly — splitting coupled functions across segments is the code-analogue of splitting a function mid-body, just less visible.
- **CSV row-count-per-chunk is derived from *measured* average row size**, not assumed uniform, because "log files and exports often have highly variable row lengths (a stack-trace line vs. a one-word status line)."

Anything `FormatDetector` doesn't recognize routes to a degraded `TextChunker` with a stated caveat — never to a crash. This single design choice directly answers the complexity risk in Part 6: adding chunker #6 never requires touching the dispatcher or the proposal flow.

#### SegmentProcessor (`teaagent/segment_processor.py` — new)
One model call per segment, with `prior_summary` carryover (compressed, never raw — see below), progress events emitted through the existing `context_bus.py` streaming-progress channel, and a `handle_segment_failure` function implementing a deliberate **three-rung escalation ladder**, never a silent retry loop:
1. **Retry with a smaller chunk** — halve the *content* portion of just that segment's budget and re-chunk only that region.
2. **Skip + warn** — a structured, user-visible warning ("Segment 5/30 skipped (timeout). Results may be incomplete.") attached to that segment's slot, visible in both the live progress stream and the final report's segment log.
3. **Abort the run** — if the skip-rate crosses ~20%, or three-or-more *consecutive* skips suggest a systemic issue, the processor stops and surfaces the pattern explicitly rather than producing a report that's silently 60% holes.

This ladder is the design's direct, evidenced response to the Claude Code "context death spiral" issue (#28863) that both the strategy document and the design cite — the clearest real-world articulation of why "just retry" is the wrong default for a context that's already degraded.

**Context carryover** — each segment's prompt carries (a) the original task description, (b) a *compressed* running summary of prior findings (capped at ~10% of the per-segment content budget, so it can never crowd out new content as segment count grows), and (c) the current segment. The design notes this technique is "already informally similar to how `CompactionManager` produces rolling summaries for context auto-compaction" — another place where the new capability extends an existing pattern rather than inventing a new one.

#### ResultAggregator (`teaagent/result_aggregator.py` — new)
Format-aware merging: JSON segment results are structurally merged using the injected `_segment` envelope (sort/dedupe/concat by known index ranges — no guessing at overlap); text/summary results go through a final reduce pass ("here are N partial summaries, produce one coherent summary, noting any contradictions"). A `validate_consistency` pass runs cheap heuristic cross-segment checks (schema agreement, rough numeric reconciliation) and *surfaces* — never silently resolves — apparent contradictions. Critically, `report_strategy` produces a plain-language explanation appended to the final answer ("Aggregated 30 segments into one report... 2 segments were retried... Estimated extra cost from chunking overhead: ~22% more tokens") — not a hidden implementation detail buried in logs.

**One refinement the strategy document adds that the design treats as a v2 concern**: `ResultAggregator` needs a defined "what did we lose" output, not just a "here's the combined answer" output — because §6.3's "silent loss" failure mode (chunking quietly drops something the user needed) is, per the evidence, the hardest failure mode to detect after the fact. The design's `validate_consistency` is a step toward this; making it a first-class, always-on output (not just a flag-on-contradiction mechanism) is the strategy document's stronger recommendation.

### Design decisions, and exactly what evidence grounds each one

This section is written carefully to avoid a trap the original synthesis brief's framing risked: claiming a tighter evidentiary link than the source material actually supports. Below, each major design decision is paired with *what the research actually says* about it — including where the link is direct, where it's inferential, and where this synthesis is making a connection the source documents did not make explicitly.

- **The 60% overflow-proposal threshold.** *What the design says*: it is "deliberately conservative-but-not-paranoid" — chosen so normal compaction can usually absorb estimation error below it, while not waiting until 100% (when a failed attempt has already burned tokens). The design explicitly calls this "a tunable... that should be revisited once real usage data exists," not a settled constant. *What the literature adds*: RULER and the Chroma context-rot study both independently argue that the *practical* ceiling sits well below the *advertised* one — which supports the *general direction* of "trigger earlier than you'd naively think," but neither source specifies 60% as a number, nor does any source derive 60% from "competitor review." **The honest summary: 60% is a principled starting estimate consistent with the literature's broad finding that advertised capacity overstates usable capacity — it is not a number the literature independently arrived at or validated.**
- **Format-aware chunking.** This one *is* directly and tightly evidenced from two independent angles: the literature (Unstructured's "partition first," `RecursiveJsonSplitter` as real shipped prior art, AST-based code chunking) and the community-sentiment findings (users explicitly asking for "chunk large files so I can access a single function," #74; "implement streaming/chunked reading," #4389). Both angles converge on the same shape, independently — this is the strongest-evidenced design decision in the whole document.
- **Per-segment audit trail.** This is *teaAgent's own existing pattern*, extended — not externally evidenced, because none of the eight competitors examined have anything resembling it to be evidence *of* or *against*. Its justification rests entirely on internal consistency (the design's mapping onto `ApprovalBackend`/`RunStore`) and this synthesis's own positioning argument in Part 3, not on external research. That is not a weakness — internal consistency with an already-validated trust model is a *strong* form of justification — but it should not be cited as "research-backed" in the external sense the other two decisions are.

### Three techniques for preserving meaning across chunk boundaries — and what each one actually costs

The strategy document's literature review (§5.2) surfaces three real techniques for preventing information loss at chunk edges, and is unusually candid that **none of them is free** — each is a deliberate trade a design has to make explicit, not a checkbox to tick:

1. **Structural alignment (don't cut mid-unit).** Cheap, mechanical, and the highest-leverage first step — this alone prevents the worst failure mode (a truncated JSON object, a function split across two chunks). This is what all five of the design's chunkers implement as their baseline guarantee.
2. **Semantic boundary detection (embedding similarity breakpoints).** More principled for prose — embed each sentence, place a boundary wherever cosine similarity between consecutive sentences drops below a percentile threshold (Superlinked's documented technique). The real cost: it requires "an embedding pass over the *entire* input before the real task even starts," which is a genuine latency and dollar tax for a CLI agent that's supposed to feel responsive. Notably, the design's five chunkers do *not* attempt this — they rely on structural alignment alone, which is consistent with the literature's implicit ranking of these techniques by cost.
3. **Overlap windows (`chunk_overlap`).** Cheap insurance against boundary information loss — carry the last N tokens of chunk *i* into the start of chunk *i+1* — at the cost of redundant tokens being processed, and paid for, twice. This is conceptually adjacent to, but distinct from, the design's "compressed carryover summary" approach (§3.3): overlap windows replay *raw* boundary content, while teaAgent's carryover replays a *compressed summary* of everything-so-far. The design's approach is more token-efficient at scale; overlap windows are simpler to implement and harder to get subtly wrong. Whoever picks up Phase 2 work should be aware these are two different mechanisms solving adjacent problems, not the same mechanism described twice.

### Three patterns for result aggregation — and the open question none of the literature resolves cleanly

The map-reduce literature (F22 Labs, source #16 in the strategy document's bibliography) is "unusually candid about the central risk here: the quality of the final answer is bounded by the quality of the worst intermediate summary, and cross-chunk relationships... can vanish entirely if each chunk is processed in isolation." Three aggregation patterns exist, and the strategy document is explicit that *none of them is free*:

- **Sequential with running state** — each chunk sees a summary of what came before. Preserves cross-chunk continuity; serializes the work (slower, and errors compound forward). This is the shape of the design's `SegmentProcessor` carryover mechanism.
- **Parallel map, then reduce** — every chunk processed independently, then synthesized. Fast and resilient to a single chunk's failure; structurally blind to cross-chunk relationships unless the reduce step is given enough signal to reconstruct them (which is exactly what the design's `_segment` envelope is for — giving the reduce step a structural key to work from rather than forcing it to guess).
- **Hierarchical reduce** — combine in a tree (chunks → groups → final), bounding how much any single synthesis step has to hold at once. Mitigates the "the reduce step itself overflows" failure mode that a flat reduce over many chunks will eventually hit — directly relevant to Open Question #3 in Part 8 (does `ResultAggregator` need a recursive/tree-reduce design for 100+ segments?).

**The sharpest open question the literature does not resolve**: what to do about a failed segment in the context of aggregation. Retry it alone is cheap but risks the same failure twice; skip it and flag the gap loses information silently unless surfaced loudly; abort the whole job is safest but throws away completed work. The design's three-rung escalation ladder (Part 4, `SegmentProcessor` section above) is its answer to exactly this question — and it is worth noting that the ladder's *shape* (try smaller, then skip-and-warn, then abort-on-pattern) maps closely onto the literature's three options, sequenced by cost rather than chosen as a single fixed policy. That sequencing — rather than picking one of the three options as a blanket rule — is, in this synthesis's reading, the design's most quietly important contribution: it turns "which of three imperfect options do we pick?" into "in what order do we try them, and when do we stop trying?"

## Part 6 — Risk Assessment & Mitigation

This section preserves the design document's own risk framing — including its unusual practice of stating, for each risk, a *residual risk the design accepts* rather than claiming full mitigation. That honesty is itself a feature of the source material worth preserving rather than smoothing into "all risks mitigated."

### Accuracy risk — does chunking lose semantic meaning?
**The evidence says this is not hypothetical.** It is *sethcronin*'s specific, first-hand objection on the Hacker News "Show HN: Context Gateway" thread — intermediary compression "can strip useful context that the agent actually needs to diagnose" — and it is structurally what the map-reduce literature calls the central trade-off of the entire pattern (the final answer's quality is bounded by the worst intermediate summary, and cross-chunk relationships can vanish if each chunk is processed in isolation).

*Mitigations, per the design*: context carryover (compressed, not raw — directly targets cross-chunk pattern tracking); `validate_consistency` (surfaces, doesn't hide, apparent contradictions); disclosure in the final report ("the answer was produced by chunked analysis... may miss patterns that span widely-separated parts of the input").

*Residual risk the design explicitly accepts*: "No amount of carryover fully replaces single-shot analysis with a sufficiently large context window." This is disclosed in the *initial proposal*, not after the fact — the user consents to the trade-off, they aren't surprised by it.

**One nuance worth adding from the literature that the design doesn't fully incorporate**: Chroma's counter-intuitive finding that *coherent* context can sometimes perform *worse* than shuffled context complicates the intuition that "preserve more structure = preserve more meaning." This doesn't invalidate the design's structural-preservation approach — it suggests that *how reassembled chunks are ordered and presented back to the model* (an area the community-sentiment document flags as almost entirely unaddressed in tooling) may eventually be as important a lever as how they're split, and is a candidate area for teaAgent to differentiate on substance once the core pipeline exists.

### Cost risk — does chunking cost more tokens?
**Confirmed directly, not estimated abstractly**: Cline issue #6667 reports real per-call costs jumping from "$0.03–$0.20" to "$2–$4" under uncontrolled context growth. The design's own estimate for the proposed pipeline is **~20–30% overhead**, combining ~10% from carryover generation/transmission and ~10–20% from format-specific structural repetition (the exact figure depends heavily on format — a CSV with a tiny header repeats cheaply; JSON with large sibling metadata repeats expensively, which the design notes is *itself* a signal that the dominant-array detection chose poorly).

*Mitigations*: cap carryover at a fixed fraction of the per-segment budget (so it cannot grow unboundedly across 30+ segments); disclose the cost estimate *up front*, using teaAgent's existing cost-estimation machinery, "so the number shown is consistent with whatever cost dashboard the user already trusts."

*Residual risk the design explicitly accepts*: "for genuinely huge inputs... even a 30% overhead on a task that wouldn't otherwise be possible at all is a reasonable trade — the alternative isn't 'cheaper,' it's 'impossible.'"

### UX risk — does automatic proposal overwhelm users?
**The evidence cuts both ways, and the design treats this honestly rather than picking the comfortable answer.** On one side: the HN "Context Gateway" debate showed real practitioners split on whether automated handling helps or hurts; Aider #4583 is a user asking to *cap* automation, not increase it; and Lilian Weng's synthesis frames "human input" as only one of three legitimate decomposition drivers — not the default one. On the other: *ivzak*'s HN comment that "steering away from literal matching crushes performance already at 8k+ tokens" argues context rot bites early enough that *not* proposing intervention has its own cost.

*Mitigations*: threshold tuning framed explicitly as "a starting point to be revisited with real usage data"; a clear, short, three-option proposal with a stated recommendation (mirrors how JIT approval prompts already work for low-stakes calls); free-form override so power users never have to navigate a menu.

*Residual risk the design explicitly accepts — and frames as a feature, not a bug*: "some friction is the point — this is a decision that changes result fidelity and cost, and teaAgent's whole trust model... is built on *not* hiding consequential decisions behind silent defaults. A small amount of 'naggy' is the cost of 'the agent never surprises you with a bill or a degraded answer you didn't agree to.'" This framing — friction as a *deliberate* governance signal rather than an unfortunate side effect — is, in this synthesis's judgment, the single most quotable line in the entire design document for positioning purposes (see Part 7).

### Complexity risk — is this too much new surface area to maintain?
The design names this directly: five format-specific chunkers, a multi-rung retry ladder, a proposal/consent flow, and a structurally-aware aggregator is "a lot of new surface area." *Mitigations*: a plugin architecture (`ChunkerRegistry` registers chunkers via entry points, "exactly the way teaAgent already registers tools" — adding format #6 never touches the dispatcher); a shared protocol with isolated implementations (bugs in one chunker cannot leak into another, and each is independently testable); graceful degradation as the universal fallback (`FormatDetector` routes anything unrecognized to `TextChunker` with a stated caveat — "works, with a disclosed accuracy trade-off," never "crashes").

*Residual risk the design explicitly accepts*: "this is genuinely more code than a 'just truncate and hope' approach... it is contained — a non-overflowing task never touches any of it — so the maintenance burden scales with how often the feature is actually exercised, not with the size of the codebase as a whole."

### Adoption risk — will competitors copy this?
**This risk is not directly addressed by name in any of the four source documents** — it is this synthesis's own framing, built on the competitive-moat argument in Part 3. The defensible version of the argument: a competitor could plausibly ship a format-aware chunker quickly (the prior art is open-source and shipped — `RecursiveJsonSplitter` exists today), but retrofitting a governance layer deep enough to make every chunk independently auditable, cost-capped, and isolated is a different order of work — none of the eight competitors examined built that layer for *any* of their existing actions, which is structural evidence it's not a quick add. The honest residual: this argument is an *inference from the absence of evidence* (no competitor has this layer) rather than direct evidence that building it is hard for them specifically — it should be treated as a reasonable hypothesis to monitor, not a guaranteed multi-year lead.

## Part 7 — Go-to-Market & Positioning

**This entire section is this synthesis's own construction.** None of the four source research documents contain go-to-market analysis, customer interviews, or positioning testing — they are technical, competitive, and community-sentiment research passes. What follows is built by combining the competitive-moat argument (Part 3, itself grounded in the competitor audit's findings) with the design document's own framing choices (which, notably, already read like positioning language in places — e.g., "the agent never surprises you with a bill or a degraded answer you didn't agree to"). Treat this section as a *starting draft for messaging*, to be tested with real users, not as validated positioning.

### A candidate core message, derived from the evidence rather than invented from scratch
**"Never get surprised by a task that's too big — see the plan, see the cost, see exactly what happened to every piece."**

This phrasing is deliberately built around the one claim the research can actually support: that teaAgent's advantage is *transparency and consent about a known-hard problem*, not raw capability to handle arbitrary size (a claim that would put teaAgent in a capability arms race against larger-context-window competitors it cannot structurally win). The "process any size task" framing from the original brief is *narrower* than what the evidence supports — the evidence says the differentiator is *how the agent behaves when it can't*, not a claim that it always can.

### Why this should resonate — grounded in what the research actually found people saying
- **For users who've been burned by silent failure** (the AnythingLLM 96K→8K collapse, the Cline cost spike from $0.03 to $2–4, the Claude Code "context death spiral"): "I see exactly what was processed, what was skipped, and what it cost — before I'm surprised by any of those things" is a direct answer to a frustration documented, in their own words, in the community-sentiment research.
- **For users who want control, not automation** (Aider #4583's request to cap context manually): the proposal-based design's "stop and let me rescope" option, always present, always honored, is a substantively different posture than either silent compaction (which this user explicitly doesn't want more of) or a hard wall (which is what they currently get).
- **For regulated-industry or audit-conscious users**: per-segment audit trail is "regulatory gold" — *this synthesis's inference, not a finding from any research document*. It is plausible given teaAgent's existing governance positioning, but it has not been tested against an actual regulated-industry user and should not be presented to one as a validated claim until it has been.

### Differentiation — what can honestly be claimed today vs. what requires shipping first
| Claim | Can be made today? | What the evidence supports |
|---|---|---|
| "We propose a plan before processing oversized tasks — nobody else does" | **No — not until shipped.** The competitor audit is explicit: this is a documented *gap*, not something teaAgent currently does differently. | Once shipped, the claim is well-evidenced — five of eight competitors examined have open, unaddressed user requests for exactly this. |
| "We have governance-first chunking — full audit trail per segment" | **No — not until built.** This is currently a design proposal (`docs/research/segment-aware-agent-design-2026-06-07.md`), not a shipped capability. | The *substrate* claim ("we already have JIT approval, audit trails, and cost tracking that this would extend") can be made honestly today — the *chunking-specific* extension of it cannot. |
| "Format-aware chunking grounded in research evidence" | **Partially — the research backing is real and citable now**, even though the implementation isn't built. | The literature review and `RecursiveJsonSplitter` prior art are real, fetched, verifiable sources — citing *the research basis* for an upcoming feature is honest; citing the *feature* as if it exists is not. |

## Part 8 — Open Questions & Next Steps

The four open questions below are taken **directly and only** from the design document's "Appendix: Open questions for implementation review" — they are the questions someone with access to the live teaAgent codebase needs to answer, and this synthesis adds only the framing of *why each one matters* and *what's at stake if it's answered wrong*.

1. **Can `estimate_tokens` cleanly wrap whatever estimator backs `build_token_budget_report`'s `estimated_input_tokens` today?** The design assumes "good enough, fast, conservative" — not provider-exact tokenization. *What's at stake*: the community-sentiment research found that broken token-accounting (OpenCode's hardcoded 200K fallback against a configured 1M model, AnythingLLM's 96K→8K collapse) is precisely the kind of bug that undermines trust in *everything* built on top of it. Getting this wrong doesn't just produce a slightly-off threshold — it produces the exact "the tooling's bookkeeping about how much room is left is itself unreliable" failure mode the community-sentiment document calls out as underlying multiple competitor complaints.

2. **Should the 60% propose-trigger and 50% chunk-size fraction live in `ContextProfile` (per-profile tuning) or be global constants?** The design leans toward `ContextProfile`, "for consistency with how `output_reserve_tokens` already varies by profile." *What's at stake*: the literature's RULER finding (advertised vs. usable context varies meaningfully by model) argues that a single global threshold will be wrong for *some* models in *both* directions — too eager for models that handle their advertised window well, too lax for models that don't. Per-profile tuning is the more defensible position on the evidence; a global constant would be optimizing for implementation simplicity over the literature's central finding.

3. **Does `ResultAggregator`'s reduce pass need a recursive/tree-reduce design for very large segment counts (100+)?** Flagged by the design as "a v2 concern, not a v1 blocker — 30-segment runs are comfortably within a single reduce pass." *What's at stake*: the map-reduce literature is explicit that a flat reduce over many chunks will eventually overflow *itself* — this is a predictable failure mode, not a hypothetical one, and the design is right to flag it now even while deferring the fix. Whoever picks up Phase 3 should treat this as a known-future problem, not a surprise.

4. **Does `teaagent/context_pack.py`'s existing LSP hydration path expose a symbol table/call-graph primitive `_cluster_by_call_graph` can build on directly — or does a narrower AST-only utility need to be added?** *What's at stake*: this single answer determines whether `CodeChunker` is mostly an integration task (reuse existing LSP infrastructure) or a build-from-scratch task (write a new AST utility). Given that the design recommends building *one format well* before generalizing (Part 5, Phase 1), and code is one plausible candidate for that one format, this question should be answered *before* committing to code as the Phase 1 target — answering it might tip the choice toward JSON or logs instead, if the LSP path turns out not to expose what's needed.

### Implementation partners and feedback loops
**Not addressed by any of the four source documents** — this is a gap this synthesis cannot fill from desk research. Identifying real candidates (e.g., teams running 10GB+ log analysis, or teams in regulated industries who would value per-segment audit trails) requires conversations this research pass did not have access to. This is flagged here as a genuine next step, not glossed over with a plausible-sounding placeholder.

- **Low confidence (flagged explicitly by the source research, preserved here rather than smoothed over):** Reddit/Discord/X sentiment (one source declined to report it at all; the other reported it as "via search index" with explicit caveats — neither constitutes verified community sentiment); any specific calendar date (none of the four research passes examined teaAgent's velocity or current architecture in enough depth to support a date, and the design document explicitly warns against manufacturing one); the "regulated industries" target-customer hypothesis and all of Part 7's go-to-market framing (this synthesis's own construction, clearly marked as such, not a finding from any research pass).

## A closing note on what this synthesis deliberately did not do

In assembling four research passes into one document, the easiest failure mode would have been to round off each document's careful hedges into confident-sounding claims — turning "a starting estimate, to be revisited" into "the threshold is 60%," or "no source commits to a calendar" into a plausible-looking Gantt chart. This synthesis has tried, throughout, to do the opposite: to preserve every place where a source document said *"this is inference, not evidence"* or *"this gap could not be verified"* or *"this is a tunable, not a constant"* — because those are exactly the statements that get lost first when documents get summarized, and exactly the statements a future reader most needs intact to avoid building on a foundation that looks sturdier than it is.

The single sentence from the design document worth carrying forward above all others, because it is both the best-evidenced design principle *and* the best positioning line discovered in this entire research effort: **"never silently truncate, never silently fail, and never block on a decision the user didn't ask to make."** Everything else in this synthesis — the architecture, the phasing, the risk analysis, the positioning — is, in one sense or another, an elaboration of that one sentence. If the eventual implementation holds to it more faithfully than the eight competitors examined here have managed, the rest of the strategy in this document follows from that faithfulness; if it doesn't, no amount of clever chunking will matter.
